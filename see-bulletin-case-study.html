The SEE Bulletin

How one weekly newsletter replaced a scattered inbox for 1,500 students and staff at the University of Leeds.

Role
Student Education Experience Officer
Organisation
University of Leeds
Audience
1,500+ students and staff, every week

The problem

Everything a student needed to know lived somewhere different.

Assessment deadlines arrived in one email. Welcome week guidance in another. PhD opportunities in a third, usually forwarded on from a team that had already sent it once. Nothing was wrong with any single message. The problem was the pile.

Students stopped opening them. When they needed something specific they could not find it, so they emailed and asked. Staff then spent part of every week answering questions that had already been answered, in a message from three weeks earlier, under a subject line nobody could remember.

That is the real cost of inbox fatigue. It rarely arrives as a complaint. It arrives as the same question, again and again, from a different student each time.

The brief I set myself

Get it into one place, once a week.

The constraints were clear from the start. It had to be quick to read on a phone between lectures. It had to hold to the University's brand standards and accessibility guidelines properly, not approximately. And it had to bring down the queries landing in the team's inbox, because otherwise it was just one more thing to send.

What I built

I proposed, designed and launched the directorate's first weekly newsletter, The SEE Bulletin.

A workflow, not a one off

The hard part of a weekly newsletter is never the first edition. It is the one you have to build on a quiet week when nobody has sent you anything. I set up a weekly cycle for gathering updates from internal teams, other universities and central University services, with a cut off point so every edition could be written, checked and sent on the same day. Contributors learned the rhythm, which meant items started arriving in a usable state rather than as a forwarded email with no context.

Structure people could skim

Every edition used the same heading hierarchy, H1 down to H2, in the same running order. Deadlines sat in the same place each week. Opportunities sat in the same place each week. Someone scanning on a phone could find the section they cared about without reading the rest, which is the only way a mailing list of this size actually works.

Accessible by default, not on request

I built each issue against the University's approved colour palette and typography, with correct logo placement, alt text on every piece of media, and text to background contrast checked against WCAG ratios rather than eyeballed. It cost a few minutes an edition. It meant nobody had to ask for a version they could read.

What changed

Open rates went from 68% to the mid 80s, and stayed there.

Before the bulletin 68%
Once the bulletin was established 83 to 86%

Average open rate across weekly editions. The lighter section shows the range the rate settled into rather than a single peak.

Sustained matters more to me than the peak. One good edition proves nothing. Holding the mid 80s week after week, with an audience that had been actively tuning directorate email out, is what told me the bulletin had become the thing people checked rather than the thing they skipped.

Repeat queries dropped noticeably alongside it. I do not have a clean before and after figure for that one, as query volume was not being logged, but the change was clear enough that colleagues raised it without being asked.

What I took from it

The newsletter was the visible part. The useful part was the workflow underneath it, because that is what kept it alive past the first month, and it is the first thing I would rebuild anywhere else.
