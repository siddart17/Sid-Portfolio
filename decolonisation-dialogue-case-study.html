The Big Decolonisation Dialogue

Building four channels from zero for a podcast on a contested subject, and having a plan ready for the days the conversation turns.

Role
Social Media Manager
Organisation
University of Leeds
Audience
Students, academics and the wider public

The problem

A podcast about decolonisation has no neutral audience.

The Big Decolonisation Dialogue examined how different development themes connect to decolonisation, in conversation with academics and students. Serious subject, serious guests, no audience. It had no social presence at all when I came in, which sounds like the easy version of the job, and is not. Starting from zero means every decision is yours and none of them are informed.

The real complication was the subject. Decolonisation is not something people scroll past neutrally. Part of the audience is deeply invested, part of it is hostile, and a university's name sits on everything you publish. Growth was the brief. Growth without a mess was the actual job.

The brief

Find the people who want this conversation, reach them where they already are, and be ready for the bad days.

A niche academic subject does not have a mass audience waiting for it. The work was identifying who genuinely cared, working out which platforms they used and what each one would carry, then turning a long-form podcast into something that could travel.

What I did

Built four channels from nothing

Instagram, X, LinkedIn and YouTube, all opened and grown from zero. The podcast was the source material and everything else was cut from it, with each platform getting the format it actually rewards rather than the same asset four times.

  • Instagram
  • X
  • LinkedIn
  • YouTube

Ran the content on an AIDA funnel

Awareness, interest, desire, action. Every post had a job in that sequence instead of being another episode announcement. Clips and hooks near the top, episode context in the middle, listen and follow prompts at the end. One episode produced several different assets for several different stages.

Let the data change the plan

I tracked reach, impressions and engagement rate through Hootsuite. The clearest finding was that short video and clips pulled straight from the episodes outperformed static graphics on Instagram and YouTube by a wide margin. So I moved production time into short-form video and cut back on designed stills, which was not the plan I started with. Visual assets were built in Adobe Creative Suite and Canva.

The part that mattered most: a plan for when it goes wrong

During a period of heightened geopolitical tension, I wrote a risk management plan for the channels. It set out what counted as a scenario, who made the call, and a template response for each case, so nobody was drafting a reply to a hostile thread at speed with the University's name attached.

Alongside it I monitored discussion around the topic and replied in threads where a straight, professional answer was genuinely useful, rather than only broadcasting. On a subject like this, moderation is not a support function. It is the strategy.

What changed

The channels found their audience, and the tone held. Nothing published required a retraction or an apology, which on this subject is a result in itself.

  • Zero to 465 followers on LinkedInBuilt from a standing start, alongside three other channels opened at the same time
  • 10,000+ organic impressionsAcross platforms, from story-led content rather than paid promotion
  • Short-form video as the lead formatA change made from the analytics, not from the original plan
  • A documented risk plan with response templatesScenarios, decision-making and drafted replies, ready before they were needed

What I took from it

The risk plan was the part nobody asked for and the first thing I would build again. A communications plan that only covers the good days is not a plan, it is a content calendar.
