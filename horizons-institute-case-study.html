Horizons Institute

Turning interdisciplinary research into web copy, blog writing and partnership guidance that people outside the discipline could actually use.

Role
Communications Assistant
Organisation
Horizons Institute, University of Leeds
Audience
Researchers, international partners and the public

The problem

Good research does not explain itself.

Horizons Institute exists to get researchers from different faculties working on problems too big for one discipline. The work is strong. The way it usually arrives, in grant language, abstracts and field shorthand, is written for people already inside the field.

That matters more than it sounds. The Challenge Theme Network pages were not there to impress anyone. They were there to get a researcher in a different faculty to read two paragraphs, recognise something of their own work in it, and get in touch. A magnetism specialist writing for magnetism specialists will not do that. Neither will a page so stripped back that the science stops sounding credible.

The brief

Say what the work actually is, in language someone outside the discipline can follow, without flattening it.

That applied across three different jobs: web copy for the Challenge Theme Networks, public blog writing, and guidance documents for an internationally funded partnership where the readers were not in the UK and could not be assumed to know how any of it worked.

How I worked

The same method each time, because the alternative is a page that reads like a rewritten grant application.

  1. Read the field before talking to anyone

    I researched each subject area myself first: what it covers, where the open questions sit, what the terms mean. It meant the interview could start at the interesting part instead of with a researcher explaining their own discipline from scratch.

  2. Interview the people doing the work

    I interviewed the project co-leads directly and wrote the pages from what they told me rather than from existing documentation. Where something was too specialist to publish as it stood, I rewrote it in plain English and took that version back to them.

  3. Get it signed off by the researcher

    Every page went to the co-leads for approval before publication. The plain English version had to be one they were willing to put their name to. Nothing went live that a researcher felt misrepresented their work.

What I produced

Challenge Theme Network pages

Web copy for the Institute's Challenge Theme Networks, including the Healthy Buildings Network, Magnetism in Biomaterials and the Polycrisis Network. I built them in Jadu, kept the structure consistent with the Institute's existing pages, and wrote to University brand guidelines and WCAG accessibility standards. Clear headings, short sections and internal links, so a reader could work out what the network was and how to join it without hunting.

Public blog writing

For the Reimagine Ageing piece on Medium, I attended the event, took notes throughout, interviewed two of the researchers involved and transcribed the conversation. I then structured the article around the field itself rather than the running order of the day, so it still made sense to a reader who had not been in the room.

International partnership guidance

For a funded collaboration with a South African partner university, I drafted guidance for incoming participants. I wrote it without UK-specific assumptions and without academic acronyms, because the people reading it were navigating visa requirements and unfamiliar academic processes from outside the system that produced them. Clear, culturally sensitive, and possible to follow without a phone call.

What it came to

The pages went live and did what they were built for. Someone outside the discipline could work out in under a minute whether a network was relevant to them and how to get involved, and enquiries were tracked through GA4.

One honest note on this one. The networks carried on growing after I left the Institute. The copy is mine to claim. The growth that came later is not.

What I took from it

Read the subject before you interview anyone. It changes what people tell you. A researcher who can tell you already understand the shape of their field gives you the interesting version rather than the introductory one, and the interesting version is the only one worth publishing.
